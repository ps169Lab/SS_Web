The Great Plains Homeland

The Great Plains stretch across the middle of North America from Canada down to Texas. This land is famous for wide-open grasslands, rolling hills, and very few trees. The weather on the plains brings big extremes: scorching hot summers, freezing winter blizzards, and powerful prairie winds. The Great Plains are huge flat grasslands in the middle of America. There are very few trees. Summers are very hot, and winters bring cold snowstorms and strong winds.

The Lakota people are one of the major groups of the Plains nation. For centuries, they adapted to life on the open prairie. Because huge herds of wild buffalo roamed across the grasslands in search of fresh grass, the Lakota lived a nomadic lifestyle, moving their camps to follow the herds. The Lakota lived on the grasslands for a long time. Large herds of buffalo moved across the grass to eat. The Lakota moved their camps to follow the buffalo.

Hunting the Buffalo

The buffalo (also called bison) was the most important resource on the plains. Lakota hunters worked together in organized groups to track and hunt buffalo. Before horses arrived in North America, hunters used camouflage and clever group strategies to guide buffalo herds toward low cliffs or natural traps. Later, when Spanish horses spread across the plains, Lakota hunters became expert riders, using bows and arrows while riding at full speed. The buffalo was the most important animal on the plains. Hunters worked together to hunt buffalo. Later, when horses arrived, Lakota hunters became expert horseback riders.

Nothing was wasted. Meat was eaten fresh or dried into jerky. Women mixed dried meat with fat and wild chokecherries to make pemmican, a high-protein food that stayed good for months on long trips. Bones were carved into needles and scrapers, horns became spoons and cups, and hides were tanned to make warm blankets, clothes, and shelter. Nothing was wasted. Meat was eaten fresh or dried into jerky. Meat was also mixed with berries to make a travel food called pemmican. Bones became tools, and skins became clothes and tent covers.

Homes on the Move

Because the Lakota moved frequently to follow the buffalo, they needed homes that could be built quickly and carried easily. They invented the tipi, a tall cone-shaped tent built from strong pine poles and covered with 10 to 20 sewn buffalo hides. The slanted, circular shape allowed strong prairie winds to pass right over without blowing the tipi down. Smoke flaps at the top let out smoke from a cooking fire inside. Because they moved often, the Lakota needed homes that were easy to carry. They built tall tents called tipis out of wooden poles and buffalo hides. Strong winds could not knock down the round shape.

When it was time to move camp, women could take down a tipi in under an hour. The long wooden poles were tied together to form a V-shaped sled frame called a travois. Dogs, and later horses, pulled the travois to carry heavy hides, supplies, and young children across the open grassland to the next campsite. When it was time to move, women took down the tipis quickly. Long poles were tied to dogs or horses to make a sled called a travois. The travois carried heavy hides and tools across the grass.
