Sharing, Stories, and a Way of Life That Continues Today

Families and Community

Inuit families lived and worked together in small camps. Survival in the Arctic depended on sharing, so a successful hunter shared meat with the whole camp, not only with his own family. Elders were respected for their knowledge about weather, animals, and travel. There were no kings. Leaders were people with skill and experience, and the group made decisions by talking together. Inuit families lived and worked together in small camps. They shared food with everyone. Elders were respected for their knowledge. The group made decisions by talking together.

Jobs were divided in ways that helped everyone survive. Men usually hunted, while women usually sewed clothing, prepared food, cared for children, and tended the seal-oil lamp. But the jobs were flexible, and both mattered. Hunters could not survive in the cold without the warm clothing that women made, and many women also fished and hunted small animals. Men usually hunted. Women usually sewed warm clothes, prepared food, and cared for children. Both jobs were needed to survive. Many women also fished.

Language, Stories, and Inuksuit

The Inuit speak Inuktitut. Before books, knowledge was passed down through spoken stories, songs, and games. During the long, dark winter, elders told stories that taught children about their history and how to stay safe in the Arctic. The Inuit speak Inuktitut. Elders told stories during the long winter. The stories taught children about history and how to stay safe.

The Inuit also built inuksuit, which are stone figures that act as landmarks. Some pointed the way for travelers, and others marked good hunting spots or places where food was stored. In a land with almost no trees or signs, inuksuit helped people find their way. The Inuit built inuksuit from stones. They helped travelers find their way. Some marked places to hunt or store food.

The Inuit Today

The Inuit are still here. Today, Inuit live in Alaska, Canada, and Greenland. In 1999, Canada created Nunavut, which means "our land" in Inuktitut. Most people in Nunavut are Inuit, and Inuit leaders help run its government. Inuktitut is one of its official languages and is taught in schools. The Inuit are still here. They live in Alaska, Canada, and Greenland. In 1999, Canada created Nunavut. Most people there are Inuit, and Inuit leaders help run it. Children learn Inuktitut in school.

Many Inuit families still hunt, fish, and share food. Today they also use snowmobiles, modern tools, and the internet. The Inuit keep their traditions while living in the modern world. Many Inuit still hunt and fish. They also use snowmobiles and the internet. They keep their traditions today.
