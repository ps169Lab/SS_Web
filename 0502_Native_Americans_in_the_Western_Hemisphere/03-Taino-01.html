The Taíno Homeland

The Taíno lived on the tropical islands of the Caribbean Sea, including Puerto Rico, Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, and the Bahamas. Unlike the cold, frozen Arctic, the Caribbean has warm sunshine all year, dense green rain forests, and long sandy beaches. However, the island environment brought its own challenges, such as fierce tropical storms and heavy rain. The Taíno lived on warm islands in the Caribbean Sea. The weather was warm all year, with green forests and ocean beaches. Big tropical storms were a danger on the islands.

The Taíno people lived on these islands for hundreds of years before European explorers arrived. Their name means "good" or "noble people" in their language. They learned how to navigate ocean waters and use ocean and forest resources to thrive. The Taíno lived on the islands for a long time. Taíno means "good people." They learned to travel on the ocean and use plants and animals to live.

Farming and Fishing for Food

The island soil was rich, but heavy rains could wash away plant roots. To solve this problem, the Taíno built raised mounds of earth called conucos. They planted cassava, sweet potatoes, corn, and beans in these mounds. Cassava root was their most important crop because it could be ground into flour and baked into flatbread that did not spoil quickly in the warm climate. The Taíno built raised mounds of dirt called conucos to grow crops. They grew sweet potatoes, corn, and cassava. They made cassava into bread that stayed fresh for a long time.

The ocean was just as important as the land. Taíno fishers caught tropical fish, sea turtles, and shellfish using nets, wooden spears, and bone hooks. They built large ocean-going canoes carved out of giant cottonwood trees. Some canoes were long enough to carry over 50 people across open sea waters between islands. The Taíno fished in the ocean for turtles and fish. They carved big canoes out of giant tree trunks. Some canoes carried more than 50 people across the ocean!

Homes and Clothing

Because the weather was warm, families did not need heavy fur clothing. People wore light woven cotton clothing or aprons, and they often painted their bodies with natural dyes from forest plants to protect themselves from insect bites and the sun. Cotton was also woven into comfortable hammocks, which were hung between posts for sleeping off the cool ground. The weather was warm, so people wore light cotton clothes. They slept in woven hammocks hung off the ground. Body paint protected them from bugs and the hot sun.

Taíno villages had two main types of houses. Most families lived in round houses called bohíos, built from sturdy tree posts, woven vines, and thatched palm leaves. The village leader lived in a rectangular house called a caney. The round shape of the bohío helped strong hurricane winds blow around the house without knocking it down easily. Families lived in round homes called bohíos made of wood posts and palm leaves. The round shape helped strong storm winds blow past without knocking the house over.
