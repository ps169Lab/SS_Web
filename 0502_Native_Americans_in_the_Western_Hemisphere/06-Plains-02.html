Bands and Family Leadership

Lakota society was organized into extended family groups called tiyospaye. A tiyospaye included grandparents, parents, aunts, uncles, and cousins who shared work and cared for children together. Because survival on the plains required cooperation, generosity was considered one of the highest virtues. A respected leader was not someone with the most possessions, but someone who shared food and helped others in need. Lakota families lived in big extended groups called tiyospaye. Grandparents, parents, and cousins worked together. Sharing was very important. A good leader was someone who shared food and helped everyone in the group.

Decisions were made by councils of respected elders and leaders. Men were responsible for hunting and defending the camp, while women managed the camp, built and owned the tipis, and prepared food and clothing. Both roles were vital, and women held great influence in family and village life. Leaders and elders met in councils to make rules. Men hunted and protected the camp. Women owned the tipis, cooked food, and made clothes. Both jobs were important to the group.

Storytelling, Art, and Winter Counts

Before the Lakota wrote books in English, tribal historians kept track of important events using a Winter Count. A Winter Count was a large buffalo hide painted with special symbols. Each year, elders selected one important event—like a harsh winter, a famous hunt, or a meteor shower—and drew a picture to record it. The hide served as a visual calendar to teach young people their history. The Lakota tracked their history using a Winter Count. An elder painted one picture on a buffalo hide for each year to show a major event. The painted hide helped elders tell stories about the past to children.

The Lakota created beautiful artwork from natural materials. Women mastered porcupine quillwork, carefully softening, dyeing, and flattening sharp quills to embroider intricate patterns onto moccasins, dresses, and bags. Later, when European traders introduced small glass beads, Lakota artists created stunning beadwork designs that are still famous today. Lakota artists made beautiful art. Women dyed porcupine quills to sew colorful patterns on shoes and clothes. Later, they used colorful glass beads to make detailed designs.

The Lakota Today

During the late 1800s, the U.S. government forced the Lakota onto reservations and killed off the wild buffalo herds. Despite these enormous hardships, the Lakota nation survived and continues to protect its land, sovereignty, and culture today. Major Lakota reservations, such as Pine Ridge and Rosebud, are located in South Dakota. In the late 1800s, the U.S. government forced the Lakota onto lands called reservations. Life became very hard, but the Lakota kept their culture alive. Today, many Lakota live in South Dakota.

Today, Lakota people live and work in many different professions while keeping their traditions strong. Schools on reservations teach the Lakota language to young students. Across the country, Lakota families gather at celebrations called powwows to sing, play traditional drums, dance in colorful regalia, and honor their ancestors. Today, Lakota children learn their native language in school. Families gather at celebrations called powwows to dance, play drums, wear traditional clothes, and honor their culture.
