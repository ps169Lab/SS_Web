Villages and Leadership

Taíno society was organized into peaceful villages across the islands. Each village was led by a chief called a cacique. Chiefs could be men or women. The cacique organized daily farming work, helped settle arguments, and led village celebrations. Leaders were respected for their ability to guide the community, but everyone worked together to build homes and harvest crops. Taíno villages were led by chiefs called caciques. Chiefs could be men or women. They helped plan work and solve problems. Everyone worked together in the village.

Life in the village centered around a open public plaza called a batéy. People gathered in the batéy to celebrate harvest festivals with music and dancing. They also played a popular ball game called batéy, using a heavy rubber ball made from tree sap. Players bounced the ball back and forth without using their hands or feet! Villages had a large open plaza called a batéy. People met there to dance, celebrate, and play a fun ball game using a rubber ball without using their hands.

Carvings, Beliefs, and Words

The Taíno believed that spirits of nature and ancestors lived around them. Artisans carved sacred figures called cemís out of stone, wood, and clay. Cemís represented forces like rain, ocean waves, and healthy crops. People placed cemís in their homes and gardens to bring good fortune and protection. The Taíno carved spirit figures called cemís out of stone and wood. People kept cemís in homes and gardens to ask for rain and good crops.

Even though Spanish colonization brought hardship and changes to the islands over 500 years ago, many Taíno words entered the Spanish and English languages. Words we use today—such as canoe, hammock, hurricane, barbecue, and iguana—come directly from the Taíno language! Many Taíno words are still used in English today! Taíno Culture Today

For many years, history books incorrectly claimed the Taíno people had vanished. Today, thousands of people in Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Cuba, and across the United States proudly identify as Taíno descendants. Genetic studies show that many people in the Caribbean share DNA connections to ancient Taíno ancestors. History books used to say the Taíno disappeared, but that is not true! Today, thousands of people in the Caribbean and the U.S. carry on Taíno heritage and family history.

Modern Taíno groups work to preserve their culture. They teach ancient art styles, tell traditional stories, honor traditional foods like cassava bread, and protect sacred historical sites across the islands. Taíno traditions remain a living part of Caribbean culture. Today, Taíno groups teach traditional art, share ancient stories, and protect historical sites. Taíno culture is still alive today.
