The Inuit Homeland

The Arctic is the far northern part of our world, and it includes parts of Alaska, Canada, and Greenland. It is one of the coldest places on Earth, though Antarctica at the South Pole is colder. In winter, the sun barely rises and the land is dark and frozen for months. In summer, the sun shines almost all day and night, and the snow melts. Hunting and Fishing for Food

Crops cannot grow in frozen ground, so the Inuit got their food from animals. They hunted seals, walruses, whales, and caribou, and they fished in rivers, lakes, and the sea. In winter, a hunter waited quietly beside a seal's breathing hole in the ice. When the seal came up for air, the hunter used a harpoon. Crops could not grow in the frozen ground. Homes and Clothing

Homes changed with the seasons. In summer, families lived in tents made of animal skins that were easy to move. In winter, many families lived in qarmait, warm houses built partly underground from sod, stones, and whale bones. Igloos, which are houses made of snow blocks, were used mainly by Inuit in parts of central Canada and by hunters who needed quick shelter on a trip. Body heat and a seal-oil lamp kept an igloo surprisingly warm inside. Homes changed with the seasons. Summer homes were skin tents. Many winter homes were qarmait, built partly underground. Some Inuit built igloos from snow. Body heat and a lamp kept them warm.

To stay warm, the Inuit wore layers of clothing made from caribou fur and sealskin, with the fur turned toward the body to trap heat. Boots made of sealskin, called kamiit, kept feet warm and dry. The Inuit wore layers of fur clothing. Sealskin boots called kamiit kept their feet dry.
